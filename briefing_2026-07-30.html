    🔍 Signal of the Day

    The Chip Supply Crisis Is Becoming a Structural Feature, Not a Cyclical Event

    Samsung's 250-fold profit surge and forecast for worsening memory shortages next year marks a fundamental inversion of semiconductor market dynamics. For two decades, overproduction and Moore's Law defined chip economics—commoditized pricing, margin compression, relentless capacity additions. Now, the opposite is true: unplanned demand (AI training, inference, data centres) is outpacing physical wafer supply at scales that suppliers cannot quickly remedy without multi-year capex cycles and geopolitical navigation (US-China tension).

    This reverses the risk profile for infrastructure investors. Chip stocks are no longer cyclical plays but quasi-utility assets with pricing power and scarcity rent. Companies like TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix face a 2027–2030 window where demand will exceed supply at any reasonable price point—enabling sustained margin expansion. For enterprise and cloud customers, this means data centre capex becomes non-negotiable and expensive. Microsoft's $130B lease commitment is not exuberance—it is defensive scarcity hoarding.

    For a 21-year-old entrepreneur, the implication is immediate: if you are building infrastructure, AI chips, or energy systems for data centres, you have a 3–5 year window of structural tailwind. Conversely, if you are building software-only plays betting on cheap compute, you face a margin squeeze as infrastructure costs rise. The bet is no longer on software eating the world—it is on hardware becoming scarce enough to command margin again.
