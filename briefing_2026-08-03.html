💼 Business & Finance

US and Japan Coordinate Rare Joint Yen Intervention

For the first time in 15 years, the US Treasury and Bank of Japan moved jointly to support the yen, which had fallen to 40-year lows (touching 160+ USD/JPY). Rather than deploying dollars, Treasury used euro sales to fund yen purchases—a tactic designed to avoid weakening the dollar and undermining confidence in US monetary stability. Japan Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama committed to future interventions if "disorderly movements" recur. The yen rose sharply on the announcement; traders now monitor the 155 USD/JPY level as the next pressure point. Source: Bloomberg, FT

Europe's Fire Disaster Costs Exceed €3 Billion; Gironde Blaze Raises Management Questions

Local residents are now interrogating early-response decisions, signaling that climate-driven disasters will increasingly trigger political accountability. Insured losses will accelerate claims payouts; uninsured losses may depress regional investment and labor mobility. Source: FT, Le Monde

🔍 Signal of the Day

The Dollar-Yen Pivot as a Proxy for the End of Unilateral US Monetary Dominance

On Friday, August 1, the US Treasury executed an unprecedented act: it sold euros to buy yen, reversing three decades of interventionist orthodoxy. The mechanical reason is straightforward—the yen had weakened to 160+ against the dollar, a 40-year low that risked triggering BoJ panic-selling of US Treasuries and unraveling the bond market. But the meta-signal is sharper: Washington can no longer maintain a strong dollar and preserve its credibility as a stable partner to allies without explicit coordination.

This reflects a deeper structural shift. For 30+ years, the US could export inflation, let its currency float upward on safe-haven demand, and discipline other central banks through currency pressure. Japan, a docile ally, absorbed the cost via persistent deflation and carry-trade unwinds. Today, that arrangement is breaking. Japan's government bond market is stressed; its yield curve is inverting; and its foreign-exchange reserves are thin relative to the size of offshore yen positions. The BoJ can no longer absorb pressure alone. Simultaneously, Trump's willingness to frame the intervention as an act of "friendship"—not technical necessity—signals that even currency management is now explicit politics, not market orthodoxy.

For a 21-year-old entrepreneur with exposure to tech/deeptech, this matters acutely. First: volatility in currency markets creates funding friction. If you've raised in dollars and operate in euros or yen, Treasury interventions will now be more frequent and potentially more disruptive than historical norms. Second: the unraveling of unilateral US monetary dominance accelerates capital controls and fragmentation. Expect European and Asian VCs to regionalize capital and reduce dollar-denominated carry trades as hedging costs rise. Third: this is a structural tailwind for deeptech sectors that can increase margins during currency volatility—particularly biotech, semiconductors, and energy tech, where pricing power and operational efficiency matter more than FX swaps.
