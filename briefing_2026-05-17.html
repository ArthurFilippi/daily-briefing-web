🌍 Geopolitics & Policy

European energy resilience insulates from gas shocks

Europe will save €5.8 billion on electricity costs this year thanks to accelerated renewable deployment, fundamentally reducing exposure to geopolitical energy blackmail that defined the 2022-23 crisis. Distributed wind and solar capacity now acts as structural hedging against supply disruptions. This marks a decisive pivot: energy independence via distributed infrastructure, not diplomacy or diversification of suppliers. Le Grand Continent

WHO declares Ebola public health emergency

The World Health Organization declared the DRC and Uganda Ebola outbreak a public health emergency of international concern, warning the rare viral strain may already be spreading more widely than detected surveillance data indicates. No approved vaccine or treatment exists for this variant. Health systems across East Africa and beyond now face pandemic-tier preparedness burdens. WHO/Bloomberg

Ukraine drone strikes deepen Moscow attrition calculus

Dozens of drone strikes hit Moscow and suburbs on May 17, killing three civilians and injuring four; Russian air defenses downed 74 drones. Escalating attacks on the capital signal Ukraine's shift toward attrition warfare targeting civilian morale and infrastructure resilience rather than territorial maneuver. Le Monde

Venezuela extradites Maduro confidant Alex Saab to US

Venezuela's post-Maduro administration deported oligarch Alex Saab to the United States after he fell from favor with the new government. Saab was tied to major corruption schemes funding Maduro's regime. His extradition signals the new administration's commitment to prosecuting regime insiders and cooperating with US law enforcement. 🔍 Signal of the Day

The AI Infrastructure Hierarchy is Crystallizing—and Western Startups Are Losing

Chinese video-generation firms (ByteDance, Kuaishou) have decisively outpaced US and European rivals in production quality and commercial deployment speed. This isn't a marginal edge; it's a structural capability gap driven by two factors: (1) integrated data flywheel from social platforms feeding training datasets, and (2) willingness to deploy GPU capacity at scale regardless of unit economics. ByteDance can afford to run loss-leading video services that improve models; US startups burn cash trying to compete on inference efficiency instead.

This mirrors a broader reordering: the AI boom's primary winners aren't software companies—they're infrastructure-adjacent firms with either capital depth (big tech), data advantages (Asian platforms), or specialized chips (NVIDIA, now Cerebras). Smaller generalist AI startups are clustering into bankruptcy or acquihire outcomes. The "democratization" narrative has stalled; concentration is accelerating.

For an aspiring deeptech founder, the implication is stark: winning in AI no longer means better algorithms. You need either (1) proprietary data moats (difficult without platform scale), (2) capital to out-compete on compute (requires €100M+ Series A), or (3) a vertical problem so specific that Western regulatory/market fragmentation becomes an advantage rather than liability. Horizontal AI software is a commodifying battleground. Europe's sovereign AI initiatives will fail unless they accept consolidation and build supply-chain depth, not more startups.
