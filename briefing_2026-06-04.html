🔍 Signal of the Day

The Quantum Computing Pivot: From Lab to Capital Markets

The near-simultaneous IPOs of Quantinuum ($1.68B) and Oxford Quantum Circuits (€301M Series C) mark a threshold moment: quantum computing has transitioned from venture-scale moonshots to institutional-grade infrastructure plays. These raises are not driven by consumer demand or clear revenue models—Quantinuum disclosed zero commercial traction—but by belief among large institutions (Honeywell, strategic corporates) that quantum will solve specific optimization, cryptography, and simulation problems within 3–5 years.

This mirrors the pattern we've seen with TSMC and foundry logic: scarcity of a critical input (quantum processing power) justifies premium valuations before demand materializes. The broader pattern is consolidation of AI and deeptech investment around infrastructure monopolies—not applications. Benchmark's $2B fund, TSMC's pricing power, quantum IPOs all reflect the same thesis: the winners in the next decade will be those who control scarce inputs (chips, quantum gates, power grids), not those who build consumer apps on top of them.

For a 21-year-old entrepreneur: this is a warning and an opportunity. The venture-backed B2B SaaS playbook (raise seed → iterate → Series A → Series D → exit or go public) still works for middleware and verticalized tools (Wordsmith, Factorial). But truly structural, AI-enabled opportunities now lie in enabling infrastructure—compute, power, quantum—which require >€100M raises and 7–10 year time horizons. Founding a cleantech or semiconductor play is harder and slower than a SaaS company, but the exit sizes (and valuations) are proportionally larger. Choose your ambition carefully.
